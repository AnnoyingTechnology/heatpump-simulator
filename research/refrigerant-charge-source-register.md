# Refrigerant charge and leak source register

Observed 2026-10-10. This register collects primary manufacturer and research evidence for a reduced-order, charge-sensitive heat-pump model. It distinguishes documented model facts from modeling implications and unknown hardware data. No numerical solver, UI, or product code was changed.

## Model-relevant evidence

### Refrigerant inventory is a state-dependent sum, not a capacity multiplier

The conventional charge-inventory approach sums refrigerant mass in the compressor, lines, heat exchangers, and other cavities. For a single-phase control volume, this is (m=\rho V). For a two-phase volume, average density depends on the vapor void fraction \(\alpha\):

\[
\bar\rho=(1-\alpha)\rho_l+\alpha\rho_v,\qquad m=V\bar\rho.
\]

The mass quality \(x\), vapor and liquid densities, local pressure/enthalpy, occupied volume, and a void-fraction closure are therefore needed to estimate coil inventory. In heating/cooling heat pumps the same exchanger switches between condenser and evaporator roles; multi-splits additionally have operating and inactive indoor branches. Inventory should follow the current thermodynamic states and geometry of those components. The equation is a standard control-volume identity; component partitioning and void-fraction correlation are model assumptions that require validation.

Oak Ridge National Laboratory's public Heat Pump Design Model exposes charge balance as an actual model mode: users can specify total charge and solve with either a high-side or low-side balance, or calculate charge from a specified superheat/flow-control condition. Its inventory settings list homogeneous, Zivi, Lockhart–Martinelli, Thom, Baroczy, Hughmark, Premoli, and Tandon void-fraction methods; the docs identify extra variable-heat-flux solutions as slower. The same input guide includes compressor and accumulator internal-volume parameters. [ORNL HPDM input data](https://web.ornl.gov/~jacksonwl/purez/hpdata.html), sections “Charge inventory / superheat data” and “Compressor and accumulator geometry values.”

The prediction is sensitive to internal coil volumes and two-phase void fraction. Harms' Purdue dissertation built detailed and simplified global inventory models, instrumented and tested three commercial air conditioners, and found void-fraction determination to be the most important inventory issue; its recommended correlations depend on flow regime. [Purdue dissertation abstract](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI3099787/), pp. 1–2 abstract. Farzad and O'Neal compared eight closures against a 10.6 kW residential system over charging conditions; Hughmark gave the best comparison for that particular capillary-tube unit, not a universal best correlation. [Farzad & O'Neal (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0140-7007(94)90048-5), *International Journal of Refrigeration* 17(2), 85–93.

For R410A, a charge-inventory study validated combined, flow-pattern-specific void-fraction correlations against measurements; the preferred correlations differed between evaporator/condenser and between intermittent/annular flow. Its two-phase coil was divided into calculation cells. [Experimental validation of void fraction models for R410A air conditioners](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrefrig.2008.11.003), *International Journal of Refrigeration* (2009). A validated R32 reversible heat-pump inventory study likewise used component-isolated charge measurements and void-fraction closures; a finned-coil model accounting for airflow maldistribution predicted heat-exchanger charge within ±10% for that 60 kW unit. [Guzzardi et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrefrig.2022.03.006), *International Journal of Refrigeration*.

**Implication for reduced order:** use a small set of zones (single-phase superheated vapor, two-phase, subcooled liquid) per active heat exchanger, explicit geometric volumes for line sets and headers, and lumped compressor/accumulator inventory only where data exist. Integrate ρV over each zone using a named void-fraction model, and sum those component inventories. A calibrated residual for unmodeled internal volume may be useful, but should be explicit and independently bounded rather than disguised as a charge-dependent capacity factor. ORNL reports a calibrated subcooling-length correction plus an “unaccounted charge” term after examining charge-variation data; that is an explicit calibration option, not evidence that geometry can be ignored. [ORNL HPDM Mark VII notes](https://web.ornl.gov/~jacksonwl/hpdm/Mark_7_Web_Documentation.html), “Improved Charge Inventory Analysis.”

The `Q` output convention needs care for mixtures: the simulator's property library may report molar quality while inventory equations require mass quality. Use the library's mass-quality output (`Qmass` in CoolProp where valid) for mass inventory, and treat single-phase quality sentinels separately. Do not use quality alone to infer inventory without void fraction and volume.

### Charge changes cause nonlinear performance changes

Laboratory results support making total charge a real physical state/input to the cycle model. In multiple tested air conditioners and heat pumps, Kim and Braun found that reducing charge by 25% caused average capacity loss of about 20% and energy-efficiency loss of about 15%; the paper notes a limited set of units and operating conditions, so these are evidence of sensitivity, not universal coefficients. [Kim & Braun (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijrefrig.2012.06.007), *International Journal of Refrigeration* 35(7), 1805–1814. An earlier residential split-system experiment found up to 6% SEER loss from only 5% undercharge and different capacity trends for undercharge versus overcharge. [Farzad & O'Neal (1991)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0140-7007(91)90027-E), *International Journal of Refrigeration* 14(6), 321–328.

Those results preclude assuming a symmetric, linear capacity adjustment. In an inventory-based cycle, charge affects the coil liquid/two-phase boundaries and subcooling, while the expansion-device and compressor controls respond. The result depends on hardware (fixed orifice versus EEV/TXV), operating condition, and controller behavior. The available studies mostly concern unitary systems, not Daikin's exact three-head inverter multisplit; numerical performance slopes must not be transferred as if manufacturer calibration.

### Slow leaks need mass and energy accounting

For a refrigerant-system control volume, total mass changes by the boundary flow: \(dM/dt=\sum \dot m_{in}-\sum \dot m_{out}\). The associated energy balance must include the enthalpy carried by that escaping mass, along with heat and work terms when retained in the chosen model. A slow leak from a specified location should therefore remove mass at the local leak-port thermodynamic state, not at an arbitrary system-average state. A quasi-steady “leak interval → reduced total charge → re-solve cycle and inventory” model is a defensible reduction when leak timescale greatly exceeds component redistribution/controller timescales; the local leak state still determines leak flow and exported enthalpy. If the model skips stored energy and controller dynamics, label this quasi-steady approximation.

Leak flow is not generally a constant mass rate: pressure and phase at the opening change as the system drains, and the port location matters. In operating R32 wall-mounted split-AC experiments with an indoor-side leak, the measured leak rate fell during the test; after one hour it was 55.7% lower in cooling and 63.6% lower in heating. Compressor discharge temperature rose while condensing/evaporating pressures, condenser-exit subcooling, and compressor power fell. These results show coupled operating/leak feedback, but their rapid-leak tests are not a direct calibration for a slow leak from the owner's outdoor service connection. [Zhang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.3785/j.issn.1008-973X.2023.10.017), *Journal of Zhejiang University (Engineering Science)* 57(10), 2086–2093.

Separate R32 experiments on a running wall-mounted system found a “fast leak” followed by a “slow leak”; flow depended on whether the leak was at evaporator inlet or outlet and on operating mode. [Yang et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2016.01.041), *Applied Energy* 185, 2127–2133. The experimental result makes a pressure-/phase-dependent opening model preferable to a constant-rate law when modeling leak mechanics. A constant selected loss rate may still be offered as a user-defined scenario, but should be labelled as imposed rather than inferred from a physical opening.

A mass-conserving dynamic vapor-compression model also requires careful state selection. Laughman and Qiao demonstrate that pressure/enthalpy state formulations can exhibit nonphysical drift in total refrigerant mass; density as a state variable removed the variation in their simplified cycle and realistic air-source heat-pump models. This is relevant if the product resolves leak dynamics continuously; for quasi-steady charge steps, explicitly enforcing the total inventory constraint at every solve provides a simpler guard. [MERL TR2016-151 / journal article](https://www.merl.com/publications/TR2016-151), *Mathematical and Computer Modeling of Dynamical Systems* 23(3), 262–283.

Leak-orifice uncertainty is large without a defined hole, discharge coefficient, line/phase regime, and ambient boundary. A physical leak model should derive mass flow from the current local pressure/phase and an explicit opening assumption; flashing/two-phase discharge may require a dedicated correlation or bounded approximate scenario. No retrieved primary source calibrates the owner's leak area, leak site, or discharge coefficient.

The most consequential uncertainty for an absolute numerical charge distribution is internal heat-exchanger volume and its operating phase-zone lengths. The most consequential uncertainty for a leak trajectory is breach location/size and local state. A user-facing model can still represent these effects without implying false precision by exposing baseline total charge (nameplate-backed), leak severity/site assumptions, and a clearly labelled inventory-model confidence range.
